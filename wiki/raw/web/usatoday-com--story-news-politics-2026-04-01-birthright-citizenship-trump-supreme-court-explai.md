# What were the arguments for and against birthright citizenship at Supreme Court?

President Donald Trump made a splash at the Supreme Court on April 1 when he attended arguments over whether his actions to restrict birthright citizenship are constitutional . But what is the dispute all about?

On the first day of his second term, Trump announced that he was blocking the U.S.-born children of unauthorized immigrants and of immigrants only temporarily authorized to be in the United States from automatically becoming citizens. That order prompted many lawsuits arguing that those children have an automatic right to citizenship under the Constitution .

Now, the Supreme Court has taken up the case, and could issue a ruling within a few months.

Here's a breakdown of what birthright citizenship is and what the controversy is all about:

## What is birthright citizenship?

"Birthright citizenship" refers to the constitutional right to citizenship at the time of birth.

When someone has a constitutional right, the only way to take away that right is through a constitutional amendment. That means Congress can't pass a law to take away that right, and the president can't issue an executive order that strips it away.

The constitutional right to citizenship at birth is enshrined in the U.S. Constitution's 14th Amendment. It says that people who are born in the United States "and subject to the jurisdiction thereof" are citizens. The meaning of that legal language is what's at issue at the Supreme Court.

## Why is Trump against birthright citizenship?

The Justice Department under Trump hasn't argued against all birthright citizenship. Instead, it says birthright citizenship doesn't apply to some major populations, such as the children of unauthorized immigrants. But Trump himself repeatedly has said simply that he's against birthright citizenship.

"We are the only Country in the World STUPID enough to allow 'Birthright' Citizenship!" Trump posted on Truth Social April 1 after leaving the Supreme Court. Birthright citizenship is uncommon globally, but the United States isn't the only country that has it .

What Trump is referring to is how most people talk about "birthright citizenship." They use the term to mean that everyone born in the United States, with some small exceptions, such as for children of foreign ambassadors , is automatically a citizen. That includes people Trump is trying to exclude from birthright citizenship: children of unauthorized immigrants and children of immigrants who are only temporarily authorized to be in the country.

## What were the arguments presented in favor of broad birthright citizenship?

Cecillia Wang, the national legal director for the American Civil Liberties Union, argued on behalf of families challenging Trump’s executive order. She said that Supreme Court rulings dating back to the 1800s guarantee birthright citizenship to "virtually everyone" born in the United States, and today, the Supreme Court should follow those precedents.

Wang pointed especially to an 1898 Supreme Court ruling in the case "United States v. Wong Kim Ark," which dealt with whether the U.S.-born child of two Chinese nationals who were permanent U.S. residents — but weren't U.S. citizens — was a U.S. citizen. The Supreme Court said yes.

That 1898 decision, Wang argued, reflects that the definition of birthright citizenship under the Constitution was drawn from English law of the time and applied to nearly everyone born within U.S. territory.

"If you credit the government's theory, the citizenship of millions of Americans past, present and future could be called into question," Wang told the justices. "All of this tells us the government's theory is wrong."

## What were the Trump administration's arguments for a more narrow right to birthright citizenship?

U.S. Solicitor General John Sauer argued in defense of Trump's executive order. He embraced the Supreme Court's 1898 "United States v. Wong Kim Ark" ruling while rejecting Wang's interpretation of it.

Instead, Sauer noted that the 1898 ruling repeatedly referred to a parent's "domicile." That's a technical legal term referring to whether some resides in a place with a permanent or indefinite plan to remain there.

Sauer said the use of that term reflects that birthright citizenship goes to the children of parents domiciled in the United States, and he said unauthorized immigrants aren't legally capable of being domiciled in the country.

While Wang argued that broad birthright citizenship has "contributed to the growth and thriving of our nation," Sauer said it created undesirable incentives.

"Unrestricted birthright citizenship ... demeans the priceless and profound gift of American citizenship," he told the justices. "It operates as a powerful pull factor for illegal immigration and rewards illegal aliens who not only violate the immigration laws, but also jump in front of those who follow the rules."
